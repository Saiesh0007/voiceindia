The problem:
India's small shops run on udhaar (informal credit). Regular customers take goods now and pay at month-end, and that credit lives in a paper notebook or only in the shopkeeper's memory. During a rush, entries get skipped. Smudged or missing notes turn into "I already paid you" arguments that the shopkeeper usually gives up on to keep the customer. Asking a neighbour for money face-to-face feels awkward, so dues pile up for months. Khata apps exist, but they expect the shopkeeper to stop, open an app, find a contact and type an amount. Nobody can do that with one hand weighing rice and the other giving change.

Who has it, where and how often:
More than a crore kirana stores, vegetable vendors, chai stalls, tailors and hardware counters, in city lanes, small towns and villages. Most are run by one person or a family, often with limited schooling and little comfort typing, especially in their own script. It hurts every day: a typical kirana makes dozens of credit sales daily, and each one is a chance to forget an entry. It hurts most at month-end, when collection starts, and during festival seasons, when credit spikes. A few hundred rupees lost every week, from forgotten entries and unpaid dues, is real money for a shop living on thin margins.

The voice-first product:
Bolo Khata, a voice ledger. The shopkeeper just says the entry the way they already talk: "Sunita didi ka 240 udhaar likh do, atta aur tel." "Ramesh bhaiya ne 500 jama kiye." "Aaj kitna udhaar baaki hai?" It understands Hindi, Hinglish and regional languages like Marathi, Tamil and Bengali. It works out the customer, the amount, whether it's credit or payment, and the items, then reads the entry back so the shopkeeper confirms with a simple "haan". It keeps a running balance for every customer. On "Sabko reminder bhej do," it sends each customer a polite WhatsApp voice note or message in their language, with their due amount and a UPI link to pay instantly.

Why voice comes first:
Shop work is physical. Voice is the only input that doesn't mean putting things down and stopping work. Many shopkeepers speak fluently and do sums in their head, but type slowly, so speaking removes the keyboard and script barrier completely. It also matches what they already do: shopkeepers already say entries out loud ("likh lo, 240"), and Bolo Khata simply captures it. Hearing the entry read back in their own language builds trust that a silent form can't. And a neutral reminder sent "from the khata" takes the personal discomfort out of asking for money. Indic speech recognition can now handle code-mixed speech, smartphones and WhatsApp are everywhere, and UPI closes the loop. Bolo Khata turns that into one habit: speak it, and it's recorded.
